# Professional Work Planning

Use this method during the Work `Plan` stage. Keep comparisons and rejected options in temporary working state; persist only the selected formal story foundation after Review PASS.

## 1. Frame the creative brief

Record the intended historical-drama type, audience, scale, short-form shape, tone, period, and explicit user requirements. Make a conservative assumption only when an omission is non-critical and label it in the internal plan. Stop when critical requirements conflict.

Keep the Work boundary clear:

- Design story foundation: premise, protagonist, theme, conflict, stakes, arcs, historical boundary, and major architecture.
- Do not write detailed Episode units, Scene dialogue/action, or Shot/camera instructions.

## 2. Build the evidence boundary

Sort consequential material into three working groups:

| Group | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Documented or adequately supported | Preserve identity, sequence, outcome, and material causality. |
| Disputed or uncertain | Preserve uncertainty; do not present one interpretation as settled fact. |
| Dramatic invention space | Invent only where it does not contradict stronger evidence or approved constraints. |

Do not change an established key outcome, insert an absent historical figure into a decisive event, reverse supported causality, or turn a disputed claim into fact for convenience. Dialogue, private interaction, emotion, motive, composite encounters, and time compression may be invented when clearly defensible and non-contradictory.

## 3. Convert event into story

Do not mistake historical importance for dramatic form. Derive a story engine:

```text
pressure on a specific person
→ goal and choice
→ capable opposition
→ consequence and higher cost
→ narrowing options
→ irreversible decision
→ climax and changed final state
```

Ask who must choose, what that person wants now, what inner need or blind spot complicates the goal, who or what can actively prevent it, what failure costs, and which decision makes retreat impossible.

## 4. Choose the dramatic center

For protagonist, viewpoint, entry point, central conflict, and climax, briefly compare more than one viable option when the material permits. Select by user goal, historical constraint, dramatic potential, and short-form fitness. Do not generate a large brainstorm.

The protagonist need not be the most famous person. Select one whose active goal, pressure, choices, thematic relevance, and audience access can sustain the Work. Use a single protagonist, dual protagonists, or a limited ensemble only when the core dramatic center remains clear.

## 5. Design agency, opposition, and stakes

Define for the protagonist and every major arc:

- external goal and concrete near-term pursuit;
- internal need, initial belief, blind spot, or divided loyalty;
- pressure, choices, consequences, and an irreversible decision;
- final external and internal state.

Design opposition as a person, group, institution, deadline, identity, historical force, self-conflict, or competing loyalty with a rational objective, coherent logic, and capacity to act. Avoid a flat villain created only to obstruct.

Specify escalating stakes across relevant personal, relational, political, social, historical, and moral dimensions. Never inflate stakes by fabricating a key historical outcome.

## 6. Form premise, logline, and theme

Write a premise that states the dramatic proposition, not merely the topic. Draft a compact logline containing the protagonist, active goal, opposition, stakes, and distinctive historical situation. Reject any logline that can only recount who replaced whom or what happened in which year.

Distinguish topic from theme. Turn broad topics such as power or loyalty into a dramatic question tested by conflicting choices. Let the ending and consequences answer or complicate the question; do not attach a didactic slogan.

## 7. Design relationship and story architecture

For each core relationship, define its initial state, conflicting needs or loyalties, pressure points, decisive change, and final state. Keep only relationships that advance the main conflict, an arc, or the theme.

Build a flexible causal architecture rather than forcing a named formula:

1. Establish the pressured starting state.
2. Trigger a disruption that demands action.
3. Escalate through decisions and consequences.
4. Introduce a major reversal that changes the available path.
5. Create a crisis that forces an irreversible choice.
6. Stage a climax that resolves the central conflict through action and consequence.
7. Show the final story, character, and relationship state.

Fit the number and density of turns to the intended short-drama scale. Enter conflict early, avoid repeated historical explanation, and make each major segment change the state.

## 8. Complete the Work draft contract

The selected plan must support a draft that independently communicates:

- creative brief, premise, logline, protagonist, goal, need, opposition, and stakes;
- thematic question, major characters, character arcs, and relationship arcs;
- documented constraints, uncertainty, and defensible invention space;
- starting state, disruption, escalations, reversal, crisis, climax, ending, and final state;
- type, tone, audience, scale, and short-form suitability.

If Script adaptation would need to invent these foundations again, the Work plan or draft is incomplete.
